• A spatiotemporal carbon intensity equalization method fairly allocates carbon emissions between power networks and microgrids.
• A multi-layer interaction mechanism is developed for carbon allocation and electricity pricing.
• A low-carbon economic non-cooperative operational strategy is provided for multi-entity networks.
• An Enhanced Action Projection MATD3 algorithm ensures secure real-time non-convex low-carbon optimization.

A B S T R A C T
Due to fundamental differences in operational entities between distribution networks and microgrids, the
equitable allocation of carbon responsibilities remains challenging. Furthermore, achieving real-time, efficient,
and secure low-carbon economic dispatch in decentralized multi-entities continues to face obstacles. Therefore,
we propose a co-optimization framework for Active Distribution Networks (ADNs) and multi-Microgrids (MMGs)
to improve operational efficiency and reduce carbon emissions through adaptive coordination and decision-
making. To facilitate decentralized low-carbon decision-making, we introduce the Spatiotemporal Carbon In-
tensity Equalization Method (STCIEM). This method ensures privacy and fairness by processing local data and
equitably distributing carbon responsibilities. Additionally, we propose a non-cooperative optimization strategy
that enables entities to optimize their operations independently while considering both economic and envi-
ronmental interests. To address the challenges of real-time decision-making and the non-convex nature of low-
carbon optimization inherent in traditional approaches, we have developed the Enhanced Action Projection
Multi-Agent Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (EAP-MATD3) algorithm. This algorithm en-
hances the actor’s objective to address the actor-critic mismatch problem, thereby outperforming conventional
safe multi-agent deep reinforcement learning methods by generating optimized actions that adhere to physical
system constraints. Experiments conducted on the modified IEEE 33-bus network and IEEE 123-bus network
demonstrate the superiority of our approach in effectively balancing economic and environmental objectives
within complex energy systems.

1. Introduction
1.1. Background and motivation
The global shift toward renewable energy is fundamentally trans-
forming modern power systems, driving a transition toward decentral-
ized energy management that significantly enhances adaptability and
sustainability. High shares of renewable energy generation are expected
to dominate future power systems, leading to fundamental changes in
system characteristics and increased demands for system flexibility [1].
In this evolving context, Active Distribution Networks (ADNs) and
Multi-Microgrids (MMGs) have emerged as crucial components for
effectively managing distributed energy resources (DERs) [2]. ADNs act
as intermediaries between the upper power grid (UPG) and end con-
sumers, optimizing energy flows and integrating renewable energy,
while MMGs are deployed within local clusters to manage DERs, thus
enhancing reliability and supporting local energy needs.
The integration of ADNs and MMGs enhances load management
flexibility, optimizes energy usage, and supports carbon emissions
reduction, making them essential for achieving both economic efficiency
and environmental sustainability [3]. However, these benefits also
introduce significant challenges: 1) Due to fundamental differences in
operational entities between ADNs and MMGs, achieving equitable
allocation of carbon responsibilities across these entities remains com-
plex. 2) The requirement for real-time, efficient, and secure low-carbon
economic dispatch in a multi-agent decentralized environment presents
technical obstacles that limit system flexibility and sustainability.
1.2. Previous research and gaps
With the widespread adoption of DERs and MMGs in modern energy
systems, extensive research has emerged aimed at optimizing economic
efficiency, system flexibility, and environmental sustainability. How-
ever, despite significant advancements in various aspects, numerous
challenges and research gaps remain unresolved. The following sections
review the current state of research in ADN-MMGs system operations,
carbon intensity calculation and application, and Multi-Agent Deep
Reinforcement Learning (MADRL), highlighting areas that require
further investigation.
ADN&MMGs operation: Recently, the ADN-MMGs system has
garnered considerable interest from the research community. Most
studies have primarily focused on improving the economic operational
efficiency and enhancing system flexibility [4]. Collaboration between
the ADN and MMGs is considered essential for optimizing the use of
distributed energy resources (DERs) under varying supply and demand
conditions [5,6]. Beyond external market interactions, ADNs also facil-
itate internal transactions among MGs, guiding users to adjust their
energy consumption through dynamic pricing mechanisms [7]. To
further enhance flexibility and operational security within these sys-
tems, local energy trading frameworks for ADN-MMGs have been
established [8–10].
While these methods contribute to economic efficiency, they often
overlook the complex interdependencies between different stake-
holders, leading to potential inefficiencies and inequities in resource
allocation [7]. Centralized implementations struggle to reflect the

Furthermore, as ADNs and MMGs play a pivotal role in integrating
renewable energy sources, the consideration of carbon emissions has
become increasingly important in their operational frameworks. This
necessitates the incorporation of carbon intensity metrics into the
decision-making processes of ADNs and MMGs to ensure not only eco-
nomic and operational efficiency but also environmental sustainability.
Carbon intensity calculation & application: While optimizing ADN
and MMGs operations is crucial for economic efficiency and system
flexibility, it is equally important to consider the environmental impacts
of these operations, specifically regarding carbon emissions. Reducing
indirect carbon emissions in power systems is essential for achieving
decarbonization goals. The carbon emission flow (CEF) model [14] has
gained significant traction for calculating carbon intensity across power
grid nodes due to its compatibility with power flow characteristics [15].
CEF has been applied in resource dispatch for ADNs [16–18], integrating
it with DERs scheduling and demand-side management. Additionally,
data-driven CEF models [19] have incorporated demand response to
dynamically adjust user behavior in line with carbon intensity levels.
Despite its effectiveness, the CEF model faces several challenges,
particularly regarding privacy and spatial equity within distribution
networks. In transmission networks, the centralized CEF model is
effective due to grid operators’ control over generators and transmission
lines, ensuring access to comprehensive data [20]. However, in distri-
bution networks, privacy issues arise because grid operators often lack
authority to access data beyond the Point of Common Coupling (PCC)
[21], the boundary between ADNs and MGs. This lack of access could
compromise the privacy of MG users, making centralized CEF models
impractical for deployment at the distribution level.
Spatial disparities complicate the issue further. Unlike transmission
grids, which ensure spatially balanced carbon emissions due to their
broad coverage, thus diluting the impact of proximity to specific power
sources, distribution networks like ADNs and MGs introduce more
complexity. Variations in user behavior and proximity to low-carbon
energy sources lead to uneven carbon intensities, undermining the
equitable distribution of low-carbon benefits. MGs located closer to
clean power sources can naturally reduce carbon intensity with less
effort, disadvantaging those further away.
This discrepancy can weaken incentives for carbon reduction. Thus,
integrating carbon intensity calculations into ADN and MG operations
presents unique challenges that must be addressed to balance economic
efficiency, system flexibility, and environmental sustainability.
Safe multi agent deep reinforcement learning: The integration of
carbon intensity calculations presents novel challenges, especially
within decentralized environments. The incorporation of carbon metrics
introduces additional variables and nonconvex characteristics, while
issues related to data privacy and fairness in carbon allocation become
increasingly significant. Addressing these challenges necessitates the
development of advanced decision-making frameworks that can operate
securely and efficiently within such complex systems. MADRL holds
great potential for solving nonconvex, high-dimensional problems
involving multiple stakeholders, owing to its advanced capability to
autonomously derive complex policies from environmental interactions
[22]. This capability makes MADRL a natural fit for the evolving smart
grid landscape, where dynamic, real-time decision-making is key to
managing distributed energy resources effectively.
However, conventional reward-driven MADRL algorithms often
overlook the stringent operational constraints present in such environ-
ments, and most data-driven approaches lack explicit mechanisms to
ensure action safety [23], which restricts their effectiveness to mostly
unconstrained or lightly constrained problems [24]. In highly regulated
settings like power systems, ignoring these constraints can result in
serious issues, such as unsafe charging or discharging of batteries, ulti-
mately threatening system reliability [25]. These limitations hinder the
full adoption of DRL in industrial settings where maintaining safety and
operational reliability is paramount.
Existing studies have explored various strategies to address above
challenges. The simple approach is to directly penalize constraint vio-
lations within the reward function [26,27]. However, this method does
not strictly seek solutions within the feasible domain, and it fails to
ensure the prevention of unsafe actions during both offline training and
actual execution. Furthermore, researchers have integrated optimiza-
tion schemes into reinforcement learning to enhance safety and
compliance. For instance, supervised learning has been employed to pre-
classify actions and incorporate physical security constraints, mini-
mizing the risk of constraint violations [28]. The interior point method
has also been used to guide policy updates, imposing heavy penalties for
any violations [29]. Another approach involves modifying the policy
update strategy to occur only when constraints are met, using tech-
niques like trust region policy optimization to handle situations where
no feasible solution exists [30]. While these methods help in reducing
violations, they also introduce increased model complexity and
computational costs, and safety concerns still persist.
To better address constraint violations, some studies have introduced
physics-shielded constraint layers in power systems, applied to various
areas like EV Charging Safety [31], PV, Battery Energy Storage Systems
control [32,33], smart transformers Volt-Var control [34] and various
energy hub components control [35]. These physics-shielded layers use
historical states as secure references to filter unsafe actions and ensure
compliance with critical system constraints. Additionally, techniques
such as action Euclidean distance penalties have been incorporated to
guide agents toward safer decision-making [36]. These safety layers
ensure feasible actions by guiding agents toward safe operational
boundaries during execution.
However, most hard constraint layers do not directly guide the
training of agent policies. Instead, they apply nearest-point projection to
ensure action feasibility and train the critic using these projected ac-
tions, while leaving the actor’s update mechanism unchanged [37]. This
approach causes policy gradients to be derived from the actor’s original,
potentially infeasible actions, whereas the critic is trained on feasible,

Based on the existing research, there are still research gaps that need
to be studied.
1) Current research often oversimplifies the diverse characteristics of
interconnected MGs and ADNs, treating them as homogeneous en-
tities [5,6], and also neglect MGs’ network constraints [12,13]. This
approach fails to capture differences in DER types, load character-
istics, and operational constraints. Consequently, existing models
may produce suboptimal or infeasible solutions when applied to real-
world environments. There is a need for more detailed and adaptable
modeling approaches that account for network topological hetero-
geneity to improve practical applicability and system reliability.
2) Centralized carbon intensity calculation models like the CEF require
comprehensive access to power flow data [14,16–18], which is
impractical in decentralized MG environments due to privacy con-
cerns. Moreover, existing approaches do not adequately address
fairness in carbon responsibility allocation. Differences in proximity
to clean energy sources can create disparities in carbon intensity,
leading to inequities and disincentives for carbon reduction. This gap
highlights the necessity for a distributed carbon calculation frame-
work that ensures privacy while equitably managing carbon alloca-
tion among ADNs and MGs.
3) While MADRL holds significant potential for addressing non-convex
decentralized low-carbon economic dispatch problems, current
methods face several challenges. Some approaches incorporate
safety measures during training to reduce violations [28–30], but
they often lead to increased model complexity, higher computational
costs, and persistent safety concerns. Additionally, other methods
lack integrated operational constraints into real safety policy during
training [23–25] which can lead to inconsistencies between learned
policies and real-world executions, known as the actor-critic
mismatch [37,38]. This mismatch diminishes learning efficiency
and policy robustness. Therefore, there is a need for a safe learning
mechanism that avoid actor-critic mismatch and adherence to
physical constraints, making MADRL suitable for deployment in
power systems.

2. Problem formulation
2.1. Framework overview
In this section, we present an overall low-carbon economic operation
framework, as shown in Fig. 1. This framework establishes a multi-level,
interactive energy and carbon emission management network among
the upper power grid (UPG), ADN, and each heterogeneous MG. The
ADN accesses the UPG’s wholesale electricity price and real-time carbon
intensity. It integrates this information with power flow data from each
heterogeneous MG to determine carbon intensity. Subsequently, it
broadcasts retail prices within the interior market among MMGs to
maximize self-utility. Given that the ADN lacks sufficient market power
to influence wholesale electricity market prices, it functions solely as a
price taker. Additionally, Since the locations of MGs within an ADN are
fixed, resulting in nearly identical market values, we assume that all
end-users MG receive uniform retail price and carbon intensity infor-
mation simultaneously. Based on their characteristics and demands, the
MGs trade energy and optimize allocations. They adjust their internal
generation facilities and flexible loads to respond to dynamic price
changes and reduce their carbon emissions.

configuration in real-world power systems.
2.2.2. Distributed carbon intensity calculation
Unlike the centralized CEF model, the STCIEM requires only periodic
updates from the PCC, which transmits transactive energy and carbon
intensity data based on power consumption measured at each interval.
In this approach, each stakeholder independently calculates its carbon
intensity using local operational parameters and broadcasts the results
through the PCC communication channels. By applying a uniform
weighted-average carbon intensity across all nodes in an ADN, STCIEM
effectively resolves fairness and consistency issues inherent in the CEF
model. Each MG calculates its carbon intensity based on local opera-
tional parameters, and the ADN aggregates the results periodically. This
decentralized approach eliminates the necessity for global real-time data
synchronization,

In STCIEM, carbon intensity is calculated through a decentralized
and distributed approach, where each MG and the ADN independently
compute and aggregate their respective carbon intensities. The process
follows a hierarchical sequence. Initially, MGs that sell electricity
externally calculate their carbon intensity based solely on their own
operational data, as they are not influenced by the distribution network.
These MGs then transmit their carbon intensity results to the distribu-

intensities, the ADN calculates its own carbon intensity, incorporating
carbon intensity and power flow data from the UPG, and subsequently
broadcasts the results. Finally, MGs that purchase power from the ADN
calculate their carbon intensity using the ADN carbon intensity and their
own operational parameters.

real-time data synchronization, thereby improving operational effi-

3. Partially observable Markov games reformulation
The mathematical model presented in Section 2 faces two key chal-
lenges that hinder a direct solution. Firstly, the potential strategies for
each entity, including the ADN’s price-setting and the MG’s energy
output setting, depend on the decisions of others within the internal
market. This interdependence results in a dynamic system, precluding
separate and independent optimization. Even if the ADN’s strategy is
determined, the optimization problems for lower-level MMGs remain
interconnected. Secondly, objective functions for both ADN and MMG
involve rendering the problem non-convex (as discussed in Section 2).
Therefore, traditional optimization methods cannot address this issue
directly. To overcome this, we use a model-free multi-agent deep rein-
forcement learning algorithm, enabling different groups to make inform
decisions online, adapt to real-time data, and swiftly formulate opera-
tional strategies in evolving environments.
3.1. Partially observable Markov games
In practice applications, information access constraints and privacy
concerns often limit agents to partial state information instead of global
observations, as assumed in standard Markov games. Therefore, this
work models the low-carbon economic ADN-MMG energy scheduling
problem as a Partially Observable Markov Games (POMGs).

4. Algorithm for formulated POMGs
To address the POMGs presented in Section 3, we introduce the EAP-
MATD3 algorithm. This advanced algorithm, rooted in a model-free
Actor-Critic framework, excels at learning decision strategies within
high-dimensional continuous action-state spaces by leveraging deep
learning techniques. The MATD3 algorithm uniquely employs a
Centralized Training and Decentralized Execution (CTDE) mechanism,
which preserves each participant’s autonomy while significantly
enhancing system-wide synergy by optimizing overall performance.
4.1. CTDE architecture
Building on TD3 [39] and MADDPG [40], the MATD3 algorithm
extends the TD3 framework to multi-agent environments [41]. This
CTDE framework introduces global optimization strategies through
centralized offline training, allowing agents to learn optimal policies by
synthesizing the state and action information of all agents. Importantly,
this centralized training safeguards privacy by using offline historical or
aggregated data that does not reveal sensitive or individual real-time
information. During online execution, each agent operates indepen-
dently based on local observations, effectively navigating uncertainties
and dynamic environmental changes while ensuring autonomy, privacy,
and flexibility. This decentralized execution guarantees that agents do
not share sensitive information in real-time, thereby protecting privacy
while maintaining system optimization.
4.2. DRL with action projection
4.2.1. Mathematical interpretation of action projection
The mathematical interpretation of Action Projection with DRL in-
volves solving a quadratic programming problem [23,28,29,34]. We
define a set C containing X constraint functions, where each ci(s, a) ≤0

projected action a’ that satisfies the physical constraints. This projection
minimizes the Euclidean distance between a’ and the original action a,
ensuring strict adherence to system constraints in Eq.(35).

4.2.2. The issue of original action projection
In MADRL, it is crucial for agents to generate actions that are both
efficient and compliant with predefined safety constraints. Traditional
methods often penalize deviations from safe actions using Euclidean
distance within the reward function. However, this simplistic approach
can lead to violations of physical constraints during training. Alterna-
tively, some methods project actions generated by the Actor directly
onto a safe action aʹ, subsequently training the Critic using aʹ.
This practice lacks a robust theoretical foundation because the Critic
evaluate the projected safe action aʹ instead of the Actor’s original
output [38]. This discrepancy causes inaccurate Q-value estimates from
the Critic, hampering the Actor’s capacity to learn an effective policy
[37]. Reliance on external action projection to satisfy safety constraints
creates a feedback loop, optimizing the Actor based on distorted eval-
uations, which reduces overall learning effectiveness, resulting in an
Actor-Critic mismatch [38].
4.3. Architecture of enhanced action projection MATD3 algorithm
To address the issue detailed in Section 4.2.2, we propose the
Enhanced Action Projection (EAP) mechanism, implemented in two
stages. Stage 1, detailed in Section 4.3.1, ensures that actions generated
by the Actor network adhere to predefined safety constraints. Stage 2,
detailed in Section 4.3.2, prevents actor-critic mismatch, maintaining
safe bounds while optimizing the policy.
The training architecture for the proposed algorithm in solving
POMGs is shown in Fig. 2(a), while the execution architecture is pre-
sented in Fig. 2(b).

5. Case studies
In this section, the proposed algorithm is validated using a modified
IEEE 33-bus network and modified IEEE 123-bus network provided in
[42], to address the flexible low-carbon operation problem among ADN-
MMG, the DER configuration is as depicted in Fig. 3 and Fig. 14. The
algorithm’s effectiveness is demonstrated through experiments in a
complex energy network environment featuring various DERs and
flexible load demands. To enhance the robustness of the model to future
stochastic environments, Gaussian random noise is introduced to the
loads, the active power of the PV generation, the prices, and the carbon
intensity of the ADN during the training process, considering that the
training data is based on historical information. The simulations were
implemented on a computer equipped with AMD EPYC 9554 CPU, 128

6. Conclusion
To address the challenges arising from fundamental differences in
operational entities between distribution networks and microgrids, we
present a novel collaborative optimization framework designed to
harmonize the low-carbon economic operational needs of heteroge-
neous network entities. The framework incorporates the STCIEM, which
ensures equitable and privacy-preserving carbon emission calculations,
enabling a fair distribution of carbon responsibilities between ADN and
MMG.
Additionally, we introduce the EAP-MATD3 algorithm, which in-
corporates an action correction mechanism to facilitate secure real-time
decision-making in non-convex scenarios. This enhancement resolves
the actor-critic mismatch issue commonly found in conventional nearest
point projection safe MADRL methods.
Experimental results based on a modified IEEE 33-bus network
validate the superiority of our proposed methods. Specifically, the EAP-
MATD3 algorithm outperforms existing algorithms by achieving a 5.2 %
reduction in total carbon emissions and a 3.4 % decrease in operational
costs compared to AP-MATD3. Furthermore, it achieves a 2.1 % reduc-
tion in carbon emissions and a 2.3 % decrease in operational costs
compared to APED-MATD3. While the benefits to ADNs experience a
slight decrease, the distribution network cleanliness metric remains
consistent with the Preto Improvement at 0.84. This demonstrates that
EAP-MATD3 maintains high network cleanliness while achieving sub-
stantial sustainability and economic efficiency. And experimental results
based on a modified IEEE123-bus network validate the robustness and
generalizability of proposed EAP-MATD3 methods.
Future work should prioritize improving the scalability of the pro-
posed methods to handle larger and more complex network topologies,
including urban energy grids and industrial manufacturing networks. By
doing so, we can ensure their effective application in diverse practical
energy scenarios, such as integrating renewable energy sources,
